How it works

The idea, the math, and how much of it holds up.

01Paul Janet and the original idea

Paul Janet (1823–1899) was a French philosopher. In an 1877 essay in the Revue philosophique he made a simple observation: we judge the length of a stretch of time against the life we have already lived. One year is half of a 2-year-old's life but only a fiftieth of a 50-year-old's, so it seems far longer to the child.

If the felt length of a year is proportional to 1 / age, early years count for much more than later ones. William James quoted the idea in his Principles of Psychology (1890), and it has stayed in circulation ever since as a neat, if speculative, explanation of why time seems to speed up as we get older.

This tool takes the idea literally: it adds up all those shrinking years and asks what share of the subjective total you have already used at each real age.

02Derivation: from dψ/dT = k/T to S(T)

Let T be real age in years and ψ the amount of subjective time felt so far. Janet's hypothesis says the felt rate is inversely proportional to age:

1 · HypothesisdψdT = kT

Add up the felt time from age 1 to age T. We start at 1, not 0, because the integral of 1/T diverges at zero, and ln(1) = 0 gives a natural starting point.

2 · Integrateψ(T) = ∫1T kt dt = k ln T

The total felt over a life of length L is k ln L. Dividing gives the share already felt. The constant k cancels, so we never need to know it:

3 · NormalizeP(T) = ψ(T)ψ(L) = ln Tln L

To put this in “years”, rescale the subjective total so the whole life is worth L subjective years, the same as its clock length:

4 · Subjective yearsS(T) = L · P(T) = L · ln Tln L

Solving S = L ln T / ln L for T gives the real age at which S subjective years have been felt. Setting S = L/2 gives the halfway point:

5 · InvertT = L S / L   ⇒   T½ = L 1/2 = √L

Finally, the year from age T to T+1 has a subjective length of

6 · One year at age TS(T+1) − S(T) = Lln L · ln T + 1T

Because the whole life adds up to L subjective years, a value of 1 means the year feels like an average year of your life. More than 1 feels long, less than 1 feels short.

03Why equal ratios feel equal

The felt time between two ages a and b is k (ln b − ln a) = k ln(b/a). It depends only on the ratio of the ages. Every doubling of your age feels equally long:

5 → 10  ≈  10 → 20  ≈  20 → 40  ≈  40 → 80   (each = k ln 2)

The log scale works like a musical scale: each octave is a doubling of frequency, and each octave sounds like the same-sized step. Under Janet's model, each doubling of your age is the same-sized step in felt time.

Lemlich (1975) proposed that the felt rate falls with the square root of age rather than age itself, and supported it with a small survey in which people compared how fast time seemed to pass now and at earlier ages. His model makes the early years count for less than Janet's: with L = 79, the subjective halfway point moves from about 9 to about 20.

The power law generalizes both. Its exponent n sets how front-loaded life feels: lower n means faster acceleration and an earlier halfway point. (Technically, (xn − 1)/n → ln x as n → 0, which is why the log model sits at the limit of the family.) One difference: the log model starts at exactly 0% at age 1, while power-law models give age 1 a small positive share.

The linear baseline is plain clock time and is included for comparison.

What the science says

These are theoretical models, not measured laws. Nobody has measured a dψ/dT curve, and the clean numbers above should be read as a thought experiment.

  • Wittmann & Lehnhoff (2005) surveyed several hundred German and Austrian participants aged 14–94. Older people did say the last decade passed faster, but there was little age difference for shorter spans such as a week, a month or a year. The decade effect grew until about age 50 and then levelled off.
  • Friedman & Janssen (2010) found much the same in New Zealand and the Netherlands: little relationship between age and the felt speed of recent time periods, with the clearest age effect in judgments about the past decade.
  • The sense that time speeds up seems to depend more on novelty, memory and routine than on age itself. Periods packed with new experiences leave more memories and look longer in hindsight. Familiar, routine stretches compress. Childhood is full of firsts, which may explain much of what Janet noticed.

One practical reading: if novelty is what stretches time, then new places, skills and experiences may slow down your remembered years, whatever your age.
