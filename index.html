Before you use this

Four things that are true of every number this app produces.

  • They are relative indicators, not probabilities. A "Strong — 62%" readout means "far along this model's dose-response curve", not "a 62% chance of anything".
  • They have no clinical validity. Nothing here is validated against measured blood levels in you or anyone else.
  • Real variance dwarfs the model. Absorption rate, enzyme activity (ADH/ALDH, CYP2C9), tolerance and mood routinely move real outcomes by a factor of two or more in either direction.
  • Impairment starts below every threshold shown. Driving ability is measurably degraded well under 0.08 % BAC, and after any cannabis dose.

If someone is in trouble right now

Alcohol overdose

Confusion, vomiting while drowsy, seizures, slow or irregular breathing, blue-tinged or pale clammy skin, or someone who cannot be woken. Call emergency services. Do not leave them alone or "let them sleep it off"; put them on their side.

Too much THC

Acute cannabis overconsumption is intensely unpleasant — racing heart, panic, nausea, vomiting — but is not typically life-threatening in adults. Sit down somewhere quiet, hydrate, and wait. Seek care for chest pain, fainting, uncontrolled vomiting, or any exposure in a child or pet.

Alcohol

Blood alcohol concentration is a concentration, not a quantity. The figure "0.08" means 0.08 grams of ethanol per 100 millilitres of blood — 0.08 g/dL, which by long convention is written as a percentage. Two people who drink exactly the same thing can land at very different concentrations, because the same amount of ethanol is being diluted into different volumes of body water.

That is the whole intuition behind the distribution ratio r in the Widmark equation. Ethanol is a small, water-soluble molecule; it distributes into total body water and essentially ignores fat. Body water is roughly 60 % of body mass in an average adult man and roughly 50 % in an average adult woman, and because ethanol is slightly more concentrated in blood than in the body as a whole, the effective ratios used in forensic practice come out near 0.68 and 0.55 respectively. A heavier person has more water to dilute a drink into and reaches a lower peak; a person with a higher body-fat fraction at the same weight has less water and reaches a higher peak.

Two consequences follow that people routinely get wrong. First, "one drink per hour keeps you sober" is arithmetic, not biology: a US standard drink is about 14 g of ethanol, which raises a 75 kg person by roughly 0.027 % — noticeably more than the roughly 0.015 % an hour the liver removes. Drinking one per hour slowly accumulates. Second, BAC keeps climbing after your last drink. Absorption from the gut takes tens of minutes and continues after you stop, which is why the estimators here treat "how long ago" as a first-class input rather than an afterthought.

Finally, BAC is a concentration in blood. Breath testing infers it through a partition ratio, and that inference carries its own error. Nothing in this app is a measurement of either.

Almost every drug you will ever read about is eliminated by first-order kinetics: a constant fraction of what is present leaves per unit time, which produces exponential decay and a meaningful half-life. Ethanol is the textbook exception, and getting this wrong is the single most common error in BAC calculators.

Ethanol is metabolised mainly in the liver by alcohol dehydrogenase to acetaldehyde, then by aldehyde dehydrogenase to acetate. The Michaelis constant of ADH for ethanol is very low compared with the blood concentrations produced by even light drinking, so from roughly 0.02 % upward the enzyme is saturated: it is already working at maximum velocity and cannot go faster no matter how much more ethanol arrives. Michaelis–Menten kinetics with substrate far above Km collapse to a constant rate — zero-order.

In practical terms the liver removes a fixed amount per hour, expressed as a fall in concentration of about 0.015 % per hour in a typical adult, with a real population range of roughly 0.012–0.020 % and higher figures in people with induced enzymes from heavy chronic drinking. That is why the descending limb of a BAC curve is a straight line rather than a decaying exponential, and why the answer to "how long until I'm sober" is simply BAC ÷ 0.015 hours once absorption is complete — a number that does not care how you feel, how much coffee you drink, or how cold your shower is.

It also means the naive one-compartment formula C(t) = (F·D·ka)/(Vd(ka−ke))·(e^−ke·t − e^−ka·t), which you will find pasted into a great many calculators, is scientifically wrong for ethanol. That equation assumes first-order elimination with a rate constant ke. Applied to alcohol it produces a curve with an exponential tail that never quite reaches zero and a slope that keeps flattening — the opposite of what actually happens. This app uses a zero-order elimination term instead; the exact expression is in The exact math this app runs.

Erik Widmark's 1932 formulation is still the backbone of forensic alcohol estimation because it is simple, transparent, and errs in a predictable direction. It says: take the grams of ethanol consumed, divide by the mass of body water available to dilute it, and subtract what the liver has removed since drinking started.

Its simplifying assumption is severe and deliberate: absorption is instantaneous. Every drink is treated as though it appeared in the bloodstream the moment it was swallowed. That makes the model conservative in the useful direction for forensic work — it tends to overstate BAC shortly after drinking, because in reality some of that ethanol is still sitting in the stomach. It also makes the modelled peak occur exactly at the moment of the last drink, which is why this app's peak-status line for Widmark refers to the last drink rather than to an absorption peak.

Where Widmark struggles: the first 30–90 minutes of a drinking session, single large doses on an empty stomach, and anyone whose r is far from the population average — the very lean, the very obese, and people with unusual body composition. Refinements such as Watson's and Forrest's estimate total body water from height, weight and age rather than using a flat ratio, and reduce the error somewhat. This app offers the standard male/female values, a neutral average, and a custom r field so you can plug in a better estimate if you have one.

The pharmacokinetic model offered alongside it fixes precisely the weakness Widmark has — it gives absorption a real, finite rate — while keeping the zero-order elimination that makes ethanol ethanol. Over long timescales the two models converge, because once absorption is finished both are descending at exactly β per hour. You can watch that convergence numerically in the next article.

Step 0 — Volume to grams of ethanol

Every alcohol calculation starts by converting a drink into grams. The density of ethanol is a physical constant, not an estimate.

// ETHANOL_DENSITY_G_PER_ML = 0.789 g/mL (20 °C) gramsEthanol = volumeMl × (abvPercent / 100) × 0.789 // worked example: a 355 mL can at 5.0 % ABV = 355 × 0.05 × 0.789 = 14.01 g ethanol // ≈ one US standard drink

Step 1a — Widmark

A is grams of ethanol, W is body mass, r is the distribution ratio, β the elimination rate. Written with W in grams the first term is a mass fraction, so it is multiplied by 100 to express it as g/100 mL. This app keeps W in kilograms and folds the unit conversion into a single factor of 10, which is algebraically identical.

// textbook form, W in grams, result already in % BAC(t) = 100 × A / (W_grams × r) − β × t // identical form as implemented, W in kg bacPercent(t) = A / (bodyWeightKg × rFactor × 10) − betaPctPerHour × tHours // worked example: 14.01 g, 75 kg, r = 0.68, β = 0.015 %/h, t = 1 h = 14.01 / (75 × 0.68 × 10) − 0.015 × 1 = 0.02747 − 0.015 = 0.0125 %

With several drinks the implementation steps through the timeline minute by minute rather than applying the closed form once. It adds each drink's full contribution at the instant that drink was finished and subtracts betaPctPerHour / 60 per minute only while the running value is above zero. That clamping matters: the closed form would otherwise keep subtracting elimination through a sober gap between two drinking sessions and report a negative debt that never existed.

Step 1b — The pharmacokinetic model

First-order absorption into a single compartment, zero-order elimination out of it. The elimination term is linear in time, not exponential — that is the whole point.

// Vd — volume of distribution, litres Vd = bodyWeightKg × rFactor // R — zero-order elimination, grams of ethanol per hour. // betaPctPerHour is %/h = g/dL/h; ×10 converts to g/L/h; ×Vd gives g/h R = betaPctPerHour × 10 × Vd // concentration in g/L, then ÷10 to report as g/dL ( = % ) C(t) = (doseGrams / Vd) × (1 − e^(−kaPerHour × t)) − (R / Vd) × t bacPercent(t) = max(0, C(t) / 10)

Absorption rate kaPerHour is taken from stomach contents — 6.0 h⁻¹ empty, 3.5 h⁻¹ with light food, 2.0 h⁻¹ after a full meal. hand-tuned Those three numbers are my reasoned picks inside the range reported for oral ethanol; they are not measured values for you.

The asymptotic check. As t grows the absorption term saturates at doseGrams / Vd and its derivative vanishes, so the slope of the curve must approach exactly −R / Vd g/L/h, which is −betaPctPerHour in percent. The app verifies this numerically on every load:

Step 1c — Time to peak, analytically

The peak is where the derivative crosses zero, and for a single dose that has a closed form. The app uses it directly; it does not hunt along the plotted points for the largest one, which would quantise the answer to the plotting resolution.

// d/dt [ (D/Vd)(1 − e^(−ka·t)) − (R/Vd)·t ] = 0 // (D·ka/Vd)·e^(−ka·t) = R/Vd tmaxHours = ln(kaPerHour × doseGrams / R) / kaPerHour // valid only when ka × doseGrams > R; otherwise elimination already // outruns absorption and the maximum is at t = 0. // several drinks at different times: the same derivative, still closed form, // solved by bisection because the sum has no algebraic root — // Σ Aᵢ · ka · e^(−ka(t − tᵢ)) = R

Step 2 — Cannabis: the Hill equation

Receptor occupancy saturates. Doubling a small dose roughly doubles the effect; adding ten milligrams to a hundred does almost nothing. Linear scaling gets this exactly backwards at the top of the range, which is where the mistakes hurt.

Step 3 — From milligrams to effective dose

Two things happen before a dose ever touches the curve: it is scaled by how the body distributes it, and it is divided by tolerance. Tolerance is modelled as sliding you left along the dose axis — a tolerant user needs more milligrams to reach the same point on the same curve — rather than as a multiplier applied to the output, which would wrongly leave the shape of the response untouched.

// THC is lipophilic: fat mass, not body weight, drives distribution. fatMassKg = weightKg × bodyFatPercent / 100 REFERENCE_FAT_KG = 17.5 // 70 kg at 25 % body fat distributionFactor = (REFERENCE_FAT_KG / max(fatMassKg, 2)) ^ 0.35 durationFactor = clamp((fatMassKg / REFERENCE_FAT_KG) ^ 0.15, 0.85, 1.30) // tolerance shifts the dose, it does not scale the response effectiveDose = mg × distributionFactor / toleranceFactor intensity01 = hill(effectiveDose, EC50_MG, HILL_N) × cbdDamping intensityPct = round(intensity01 × 100)

hand-tuned The exponents 0.35 and 0.15, the 17.5 kg reference, the 0.85–1.30 clamp and every EC50 below are my reasoned estimates. They are shaped to behave correctly — more fat mass lowers the acute peak slightly and lengthens the tail — but no published constant says 0.35.

Step 6 — The time course

Cannabis intensity is drawn as an explicitly asymmetric curve: a climb that accelerates, then a decline with a long tail. Symmetric bell curves are wrong in both directions — they come up too fast and finish too early.

Step 7 — Alcohol's place on the shared scale

Alcohol has no EC50 here; BAC is already a physical quantity and reporting a fabricated "receptor occupancy" for it would be dishonest. To place a BAC on the same six-step vocabulary as the cannabis modules, the app uses an explicit, declared display mapping:

// display convention only — NOT a pharmacological claim scalePosition = 100 × (bacPercent / 0.30) ^ 0.75 // clamped to 0…100 // which places the common landmarks at: // 0.02 % → 13 Mild 0.08 % → 37 Moderate // 0.05 % → 26 Mild 0.15 % → 59 Strong // 0.10 % → 44 Moderate 0.25 % → 87 Extremely Strong

hand-tuned The 0.30 % anchor and the 0.75 exponent are chosen so that the familiar legal and clinical landmarks fall in sensible bands. The BAC number itself — the one you should actually read — is never transformed.

What is standard, and what is mine

Everything in Steps 0, 1a, 1b, 1c and 2 is textbook pharmacology: ethanol density, the Widmark equation, zero-order ethanol elimination, the analytical time-to-peak, and the Hill equation. Everything in Steps 3 through 7 uses those standard forms but is parameterised with numbers I chose. The complete itemised list is in Every constant I invented.

Cannabis

Anything absorbed from the small intestine drains into the hepatic portal vein and is routed through the liver before it reaches the rest of the body. For THC that detour is transformative rather than merely wasteful. Hepatic CYP2C9 hydroxylates Δ9-THC at the 11 position to produce 11-hydroxy-THC, a metabolite that is itself strongly psychoactive — generally reported as crossing the blood–brain barrier more readily than the parent molecule and as more potent at CB1.

So an edible does not deliver a weaker, slower version of a smoked dose. It delivers a different mixture. Inhaled THC produces a high plasma ratio of THC to 11-hydroxy-THC; a swallowed dose can invert that ratio entirely. This is the honest mechanistic answer to "why do edibles feel so different?" — it is not folklore about "body highs", it is a different active compound at different proportions.

The same detour also destroys most of the dose. Oral bioavailability of THC is usually quoted somewhere in the range of 4–20 %, varying enormously with the food in your stomach, the fat content of the product and your own enzyme activity. A 10 mg gummy does not put 10 mg of THC into your blood; it puts perhaps half a milligram to two milligrams of THC plus a meaningful quantity of a more potent metabolite. Those two errors point in opposite directions, which is exactly why this app does not try to convert edible milligrams into blood concentrations at all. It works in labelled milligrams swallowed, with an EC50 calibrated to that unit.

CYP2C9 is also notably polymorphic. Carriers of reduced-function variants clear THC considerably more slowly and have been reported to reach substantially higher exposure from identical oral doses. That is a real, common, and completely invisible source of the "same gummy, wildly different night" experience — and it is one of several reasons no estimator can be personalised from weight and body fat alone.

Three sequential bottlenecks separate swallowing from feeling anything: gastric emptying, intestinal absorption of a fat-soluble molecule into lymph and portal blood, and hepatic first-pass conversion. Each adds tens of minutes. Typical onset lands between 30 and 90 minutes, the peak between 1.5 and 3 hours, and the whole arc runs 6 to 10 hours — with a residual dullness that can outlast the day.

Product format shifts the first bottleneck measurably, which is why it is an input in the estimator. A tincture held under the tongue partly bypasses the stomach through sublingual absorption and comes on soonest. Nano-emulsified drinks are engineered to absorb quickly and are next. Gummies and chocolates must dissolve first. Hard capsules with a shell to break down are slowest. Food matters at least as much: an empty stomach empties fastest and gives the quickest onset, while a fatty meal delays onset substantially and increases total absorption, because dietary fat improves the uptake of a lipophilic drug.

The dangerous consequence is the re-dose. At 45 minutes you feel nothing; you take a second gummy; at 90 minutes both arrive at once. You have not doubled your experience in a linear way — but you have moved from a dose where the Hill curve is steep to a dose where anxiety, cognitive disruption and coordination loss are climbing far faster than euphoria. Nearly every edible emergency-department visit is this exact story.

The rule that follows is not a slogan; it falls straight out of the timing model: take a dose, then wait two full hours before deciding it was not enough. The estimator draws the dashed onset and peak markers precisely so that the waiting interval is visible rather than abstract.

There is a fixed number of CB1 receptors in your brain. A drug's effect depends on the fraction of them occupied, and occupancy follows the same saturating mathematics as any binding equilibrium. The Hill (or Emax) equation captures it in one line: response = D^n / (EC50^n + D^n).

Two parameters do all the work. EC50 is the dose producing half the maximum modelled response — it sets where on the dose axis the interesting part of the curve sits. n, the Hill coefficient, sets how steeply the curve rises through that midpoint; values above 1 give the characteristic S-shape of a cooperative or amplified system.

The behaviour this produces is the entire reason the app uses it. Near the bottom of the curve the response is almost proportional to dose, so 10 mg really does feel about twice 5 mg. Near the top the curve flattens, so 110 mg is barely distinguishable from 100 mg no matter what the label says. A calculator that scales linearly tells a user that tripling a large dose triples the effect — that is biologically false, and it is false in the direction that encourages taking more.

Saturation is not the same as safety. The subjective high plateaus, but the effects that ride different curves do not stop climbing in step: anxiety, tachycardia, vomiting and profound disorientation keep increasing with dose long after euphoria has flattened. That is why each effect bar in this app has its own EC50 rather than being a fixed fraction of one overall score, and why the anxiety curves are given both a higher midpoint and a steeper coefficient.

Cannabis tolerance is not psychological. Regular THC exposure causes measurable downregulation and desensitisation of CB1 receptors — imaging studies in daily users show substantially reduced cortical CB1 availability compared with non-users. Fewer available receptors means a given dose occupies a smaller fraction of a smaller pool, and the whole dose-response relationship shifts.

The encouraging half of the same finding is that it largely reverses. CB1 availability recovers substantially over roughly two to four weeks of abstinence, with the fastest recovery in the first few days. A "tolerance break" is a real pharmacological intervention, not a folk remedy.

How tolerance is modelled matters. This app divides the dose before it reaches the curve — effectiveDose = mg × distributionFactor / toleranceFactor — which slides a tolerant user leftward along the same curve. The alternative, multiplying the final percentage by some fraction, would be wrong in a specific and misleading way: it would preserve the shape of the response, implying that a tolerant user at a large dose still sits on the same steep part of the curve as a naive user. In reality they are further left, where the curve is shallower, which is exactly why tolerance is so difficult to out-dose and why escalation runs away.

The tolerance multipliers offered here — 1.0 for none, up to 4.0 for heavy daily use — are hand-tuned. They reproduce the widely-reported clinical picture that experienced daily users need several times the dose a naive user needs for comparable effect, but no study hands you the number 2.8.

Δ8-THC and Δ9-THC are isomers: identical formula, C21H30O2, differing only in the position of one double bond in the cyclohexene ring. That single displacement lowers binding affinity at the CB1 receptor, and the consistent report — from the mid-twentieth-century human studies onward and from a very large body of modern user reporting — is that Δ8 is meaningfully weaker milligram for milligram, commonly framed as somewhere between half and three-quarters as potent.

This app expresses that difference where it belongs: in the EC50. The Delta-8 module carries a higher midpoint than the Delta-9 module, so identical milligrams land lower on the identical scale, and the two reference tables genuinely disagree at the same dose rather than being the same rows with a different heading. The exact values, and the ratio they imply, are printed in the comparison table at the bottom of the Delta-8 section.

The qualitative reports differ too, and consistently enough to model: less anxiety, less cognitive disruption, a clearer head at comparable subjective intensity. That shows up here as shifted per-effect weights rather than as a different overall curve. hand-tuned — there is far less controlled human data on Δ8 than on Δ9, and reasoning from user reports is exactly the kind of inference that deserves a flag.

The part that has nothing to do with pharmacology is the part most worth knowing. Hemp contains only trace Δ8, so commercial product is made by acid-catalysed isomerisation of CBD. That synthesis can leave reaction by-products and residual reagents behind, and in many places these products sit outside the testing regime that applies to licensed cannabis. The FDA has published adverse-event and poison-centre data on exactly this, linked in Further reading. The practical implication for this estimator is blunt: the milligrams on a Δ8 label are less trustworthy than the milligrams on a regulated Δ9 label, and a model can only ever be as good as the number you feed it.

Combustion is a lossy delivery system. Of the THC in a joint, a large share is destroyed by pyrolysis, lost in sidestream smoke between puffs, or exhaled before it can cross the alveolar membrane. Systemic delivery from smoking is usually quoted in the range of 10–35 % of the THC in the material, and varies with technique as much as with hardware.

Vaporisation improves on that by heating below the point of combustion — less THC is destroyed, and there is no sidestream loss while the device is idle. Concentrates improve on it again by starting from a far higher THC fraction and delivering a large dose in a single inhalation. The efficiencies used by the estimator — roughly 30 % for a joint, 45 % for a dry-herb vaporiser, 55 % for a dab — are hand-tuned midpoints of those published ranges, then multiplied by an inhalation-depth factor, because hold time and breath volume genuinely change absorption.

What inhalation buys pharmacologically is speed and control. Arterial THC concentration peaks within minutes of the last puff; subjective effects follow a few minutes behind and peak in the 8–20 minute range. That lag between "dose taken" and "effect felt" is short enough to be intuitive, which is why inhaled overconsumption is comparatively rare while edible overconsumption is common — the feedback loop closes before you can take much more.

The trade-off is the route itself. Smoke is smoke: combustion products, tar and particulates reach lung tissue regardless of which plant produced them. Vaporisation reduces but does not eliminate that exposure, and concentrates deliver doses that make tolerance escalate quickly. Speed of onset is a harm-reduction advantage for dosing and a risk in its own right for everything else.

Lumping the minor cannabinoids into one "entourage" slider is a modelling shortcut that erases the only interesting thing about them: they do different things. This app gives each one a specific, separate action.

CBD is not psychoactive in the THC sense and has low direct affinity for CB1. It appears to act largely as a negative allosteric modulator at CB1 and through several non-cannabinoid targets, which is consistent with the common report that a high-CBD product produces a softer, less overwhelming version of the same dose. It is modelled here as a modest damping of overall intensity that saturates — the ratio matters, and past a point more CBD stops helping.

CBN is largely a degradation product of THC — it is what old cannabis is full of. Its reputation as sedating is more reliably reported than it is proven, and the sedation in aged flower may owe as much to changed terpene content as to CBN itself. Its action here is narrow and specific: it raises sedation and sleepiness and touches nothing else.

CBG is the biosynthetic precursor from which THC and CBD are both made, and it is usually present in only small quantities. It is most often described as clear-headed, with anxiolytic character. Its action here is equally narrow: it reduces anxiety and paranoia and leaves the rest of the profile alone.

All four coefficients that control these three effects are hand-tuned. The human evidence for CBN and CBG in particular is thin, and the honest statement is that the directions here are well supported by report while the magnitudes are mine.

The two substances in this app distribute into opposite compartments, and that single fact explains most of the difference in how they behave.

Ethanol is small and water-soluble. It equilibrates into total body water and effectively ignores adipose tissue. So the relevant scaling variable is lean mass: at equal body weight, the leaner person has more water, dilutes a drink further, and peaks lower. This is why Widmark's r differs by sex — average body composition differs — and why using total body weight alone systematically misestimates people at either end of the composition range.

THC is strongly lipophilic. It partitions rapidly out of blood into fatty tissue, which acts as a reservoir: it lowers the acute plasma peak a little, then releases the drug slowly back into circulation for a long time afterwards. That reservoir is the reason cannabis metabolites are detectable for weeks in frequent users while the intoxication lasted hours, and it is the reason this app drives the cannabis distribution term from body-fat percentage rather than weight. A heavier person is not automatically less affected by a gummy; a person with more fat mass is modelled as slightly lower-peaking and slightly longer-lasting.

Because most people do not know their body-fat percentage, the field is optional and falls back to a population average — 22 % for the male-average option, 30 % for the female-average option, 26 % for the neutral one. hand-tuned These are round numbers near commonly cited adult averages, not measurements, and if you know your actual figure you should type it in. The model clamps fat mass at a 2 kg floor so that an extreme entry cannot produce a nonsensical division.

One warning about the tail this produces: a longer modelled duration is not a longer high. It reflects slow redistribution, which manifests as residual dullness and next-day grogginess far more than as continued intoxication.

"Coffee sobers you up." It does not. Caffeine is a stimulant that makes an intoxicated person a more alert intoxicated person. Elimination is enzyme-limited and constant; nothing you drink changes ADH's maximum velocity. The documented danger is that caffeine masks sedation well enough that people who would otherwise have stopped decide they are fine to drive. Cold showers, fresh air, food after the fact and "walking it off" all fail for the same reason.

"You can sweat out an edible." No. A negligible fraction of either ethanol or THC leaves through sweat or breath; both are cleared by hepatic metabolism. Exercise raises heart rate and mobilises fat, which if anything can transiently raise circulating THC rather than lower it. Once an edible is absorbed there is no accelerator pedal — only time.

"Eating first means it won't affect me." Food slows gastric emptying, which lowers and delays the peak for alcohol. That is genuinely useful. It does not reduce the total dose — all of it still arrives. For edibles a fatty meal does something worse than nothing: it increases absorption of a lipophilic drug while delaying its onset, which is precisely the combination that causes re-dosing.

"Beer is lighter than spirits." The unit is grams of ethanol; the packaging is irrelevant. A 355 mL beer at 5 % and a 44 mL shot at 40 % are both about 14 g. A 473 mL craft can at 7 % is closer to two standard drinks, which is why every preset in this app shows its computed grams rather than a "drink count".

"I've built tolerance, so I'm safer to drive." Tolerance blunts the subjective signal much more than it restores reaction time, divided attention and tracking. A tolerant person is an impaired person who feels fine — the worst of both.

"It's been an hour per drink, so I'm at zero." A standard drink raises a typical adult by roughly 0.02–0.03 %, and the liver removes about 0.015 % per hour. One drink per hour accumulates steadily. The threshold panel in the alcohol section exists to make that arithmetic concrete.

"A calculator said I'm under the limit." Including this one. Every model here carries error bars wide enough to cross any legal threshold you care about, and that is before accounting for enzyme polymorphism, medication interactions, illness or sleep. There is no BAC estimate that makes driving after drinking a reasonable decision.

About these numbers

Every gauge and every effect bar in this app reports a number between 0 and 100 with a word attached. Both halves need explaining, because the obvious readings of them are wrong.

The percentage is a position on a modelled dose-response curve. For the cannabis modules it is the Hill-equation output — the fraction of the maximum modelled response for that specific effect — multiplied by 100. "Euphoria 64 %" means "this dose sits at 64 % of the way to the modelled ceiling for euphoria". It does not mean a 64 % chance of feeling euphoric, it is not 64 % of any measurable quantity in your body, and two different effects reading 64 % are not claimed to be equally likely or equally strong in absolute terms. They are relative, illustrative indicators.

The word comes from one shared six-step scale, applied identically everywhere — gauges, effect bars, and every reference table in every section. The bands are fixed:

That consistency is deliberate. If "Moderate" meant one thing on the Delta-9 table and something slightly different on the Delta-8 table, the two tables could not be compared, which is the only reason to have both. The label is produced by a single function that every section calls, so the vocabulary cannot drift.

Alcohol is the exception that proves the point. BAC is a real physical concentration, so the app reports it directly and never dresses it up as a percentage of anything. To place it on the shared scale for the gauge it applies an explicitly declared display mapping, spelled out in Step 7 of the formula article. The BAC figure itself is untransformed.

Finally, the ceiling is a modelling artifact, not a limit on experience. "Extremely Strong — 96 %" does not mean the strongest possible experience; it means the curve has flattened so completely that the model can no longer tell doses apart. Beyond that point more milligrams still make things worse — just not in the direction the gauge is measuring.

Two kinds of number live in this app, and they deserve very different levels of trust. This table is generated from the same registry the estimators read, so it cannot fall out of date with the code.

standard means an established formula or a measured physical or physiological constant, with a citable origin. hand-tuned means I picked the number: usually a midpoint of a published range, sometimes a shape chosen so the model behaves sensibly at the edges, occasionally a pure display convention. Every hand-tuned value is flagged the same way in the interface wherever it is used.

If you disagree with one of my numbers, you are probably right to — the correct response is to treat the output as a shape rather than a value, and to notice which direction a different constant would move it.

It cannot tell you whether you are safe to drive. Not "it is not intended to" — it cannot. Real BAC estimates from good models routinely differ from measured values by enough to cross a legal threshold in either direction, and cannabis impairment has no equivalent of a BAC number at all. Blood THC correlates poorly with driving impairment, which is why per-se THC limits are scientifically contested. If you have used either substance, the answer is: do not drive.

It cannot account for what it cannot see. Medication interactions are the biggest single omission. Ethanol with benzodiazepines, opioids, sleep aids or sedating antihistamines produces respiratory depression far beyond what either produces alone, and that combination is a major cause of overdose deaths. THC interacts with warfarin and others through CYP inhibition. Liver disease, pancreatitis, pregnancy, eating disorders, dehydration, sleep deprivation and acute illness all change the picture, and none of them is an input here.

It cannot model your enzymes. ADH and ALDH variants — including the ALDH2 variant common in East Asian populations that causes flushing and much higher acetaldehyde exposure — and CYP2C9 variants for THC produce large, common, individually invisible differences. Chronic heavy drinking induces CYP2E1 and raises elimination rate; that is tolerance, not safety.

It has no opinion about you. Personal or family history of substance use disorder, of psychosis or schizophrenia (where cannabis carries specific, documented risk), or of anxiety disorders changes what a given dose means for an individual in ways no dose-response curve captures. Cannabis use disorder is real and develops in a meaningful fraction of regular users, with higher risk from high-potency products and from use that starts in adolescence.

And it has no idea whether your label is accurate. Every cannabis estimate here starts from the milligrams printed on a package. Label accuracy varies by product and jurisdiction, and is weakest in exactly the least-regulated corner of the market.

What the app can do is show you the shape of a relationship — that absorption delays peaks, that elimination is linear, that dose-response saturates, that tolerance slides you along a curve rather than turning down a volume knob. Those shapes are robust even when the numbers are not, and they are the part worth carrying away.
